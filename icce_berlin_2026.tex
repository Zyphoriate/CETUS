\begin{abstract}
Accurate time-to-empty (TTE) prediction is critical for smartphone user experience, yet existing methods suffer from complementary limitations: statistical estimators fail under non-linear loads, data-driven models degrade under distributional shift, and physics-based approaches typically model the battery cell in isolation, ignoring the peripheral components that dominate runtime power consumption. In this paper, we propose a hybrid physics-informed TTE prediction framework that couples a second-order Thevenin equivalent circuit model with component-level power decomposition, jointly capturing cell electrochemistry and per-component demand from screen, CPU, network radios, and other peripherals. Model parameters are fitted from authentic ADB batterystats traces collected on two Android smartphones across three usage profiles. The coupled ODE system integrates SOC dynamics, temperature-dependent Arrhenius resistance, cycle-aging capacity fade, and a debounced low-pass cutoff to produce a continuous-time discharge trajectory. On held-out test traces, the proposed model achieves a mean TTE MAE of 0.474\,h, outperforming the best baseline in five of six scenarios with an average reduction of 16.7\%. Ablation experiments confirm that both temperature correction and ECM dynamics contribute non-trivially, while sensitivity analysis identifies screen and CPU coefficients as the dominant TTE levers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Definition and Related Works}

\subsection{Problem Definition}
\label{subsec:problem}

The input to the proposed time-to-empty (TTE) predictor consists of the following three classes:

\textbf{Battery measurements} $M_{\mathrm{batt}}$: Instantaneous state-of-charge (SOC), terminal voltage $V_t$, and battery temperature $T_b$, sampled at the on-device fuel-gauge update rate.

\textbf{Device specifications} $S_{\mathrm{dev}}$: Nominal cell capacity $Q_{\max}$ and the OS-enforced cutoff SOC, $\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{cut}}$, below which the device is forced to shut down.

\textbf{Workload information} $W_{\mathrm{app}}$: The active usage scenario together with optional per-component system features $\{x_k(t)\}$, such as screen brightness, CPU utilisation, and network activity.

At time $t$, the input to the predictor can be expressed as the tuple
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}(t) = \bigl\{\, M_{\mathrm{batt}}(t),\; S_{\mathrm{dev}},\; W_{\mathrm{app}}(t)\,\bigr\},
    \label{eq:input_tuple}
\end{equation}
and the output is the predicted local TTE together with auxiliary battery-state estimates (terminal voltage and temperature trajectories).

In this paper, we focus on the partial-discharge regime, where the prediction is issued from an arbitrary intermediate SOC rather than only from a fully charged state. Let $\Delta$ be a short look-ahead horizon over which the SOC is assumed to decay approximately linearly. The local SOC depletion rate is defined as
\begin{equation}
    r(t) = \frac{\mathrm{SOC}(t) - \mathrm{SOC}(t + \Delta)}{\Delta},
    \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
and the local TTE is obtained by linear extrapolation from the present SOC down to the cutoff:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TTE}_{\mathrm{local}}(t) = \frac{\mathrm{SOC}(t) - \mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{cut}}}{r(t)}.
    \label{eq:tte_local}
\end{equation}

Based on the above formulation, we define the problem addressed in this paper as follows:

\textit{Definition 1:} The smartphone TTE prediction problem is to estimate, at any time $t$ during a partial-discharge session, the local time-to-empty $\mathrm{TTE}_{\mathrm{local}}(t)$ in \eqref{eq:tte_local} from the input tuple $\mathcal{I}(t)$ in \eqref{eq:input_tuple}.

In particular, the goal is to obtain $\mathrm{TTE}_{\mathrm{local}}(t)$ predictions that remain accurate across heterogeneous devices, workloads, and intermediate SOC levels.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{subsec:related_work}

With the increasing demand for accurate battery life prediction on consumer electronics, many TTE and SOC estimation methods have been proposed, and these methods can be classified into three main categories: statistical, data-driven, and physics-model-based.

\textbf{Statistical methods} estimate TTE from simple heuristics using historical SOC time series. Linear Drain extrapolates the remaining time from the average SOC decline rate, and Average Power divides the remaining energy by the mean power draw over a recent window. These methods are computationally lightweight and require no model training. However, since they rely on linearity and stationarity assumptions, prediction accuracy degrades under non-linear discharge profiles and rapidly varying workloads.

\textbf{Data-driven methods} leverage machine learning to capture complex discharge patterns from real-world usage traces. \cite{electronics13244897} builds personalized deep learning models, including DNN and LSTM architectures, using real-time user behavior, app usage, and system sensor data to predict smartphone battery consumption. \cite{10.1145/3654777.3676437} adopts data-driven learning strategies to fit battery discharge curves and remaining capacity prediction directly from field-collected data. \cite{li2018predictingsmartphonebatterylife} employs large-scale fine-grained smartphone usage traces together with survival-analysis techniques, addressing data-missing problems via the concordance index. These methods benefit from adaptability to heterogeneous user patterns. Nevertheless, they require large volumes of labelled data for training, and prediction accuracy may be degraded when the test distribution deviates significantly from the training distribution.

\textbf{Physics-model-based methods} construct explicit electrochemical or equivalent-circuit representations of the battery and estimate internal states through filtering or observation. \cite{BAI2022108751} proposes an adaptive dual extended Kalman filter based on a temperature-adaptive dual-polarization equivalent circuit model, enabling robust SOC estimation under varying ambient temperatures. \cite{HE2024234718} designs an extended sliding mode observer built on a fused voltage dynamics model to achieve real-time SOC estimation with low computational cost for electric vehicle batteries. \cite{10.3389/fenrg.2022.998002} applies an unscented Kalman filter to a second-order RC equivalent circuit model, demonstrating improved SOC estimation accuracy over the conventional extended Kalman filter. Physics-based methods offer strong generalization across operating conditions since they encode the underlying electrochemistry. However, they typically require precise knowledge of cell parameters that vary with temperature and aging, and incur higher computational cost than statistical alternatives.

Based on the above discussion, the existing approaches exhibit complementary limitations: statistical methods fail under non-linear loads, data-driven methods suffer from distributional shift, and physics-based methods that model only the battery cell ignore the heterogeneous power draw from peripheral components such as the screen, CPU, GPS, and radios. In this paper, we propose a hybrid framework whose innovations are threefold. First, we ground the predictor in \textit{authentic data} obtained from both open-source repositories and on-device traces collected from real smartphones, rather than relying on synthetic discharge curves. Second, we extend physics-based modelling \textit{beyond the battery cell} by jointly modelling the peripheral components that dominate runtime power consumption, so that the generalization of physics-based models is preserved while heterogeneous workloads are captured at the component level. Third, we conduct \textit{ablation analyses} that isolate the contribution of each modelling element (temperature correction, ECM dynamics, and per-component power decomposition), thereby quantifying the role of each subsystem in the final TTE accuracy.



\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:model}

The proposed model couples four subsystems: SOC dynamics, a second-order Thevenin ECM, a constant-power load constraint, and temperature/aging modulation. The method proceeds in five steps.

\textbf{Step 1: Battery-cell electrochemical model.}

\textit{Step 1.1: SOC dynamics.} Let $z(t) \in [0, 1]$ be the SOC, $I(t)$ the discharge current, and $N$ the cycle count. SOC evolves via Coulomb counting:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz}{dt} = -\frac{I(t)}{Q_{\max,\mathrm{eff}}(T_b,\, N)},
    \label{eq:soc}
\end{equation}
where the effective capacity is
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\max,\mathrm{eff}}(T_b, N)
    = Q_{\max}(t)\;\phi_{\mathrm{temp}}(T_b)\;\phi_{\mathrm{age}}(N, T_b),
    \label{eq:qeff}
\end{equation}
with $\phi_{\mathrm{temp}}$ and $\phi_{\mathrm{age}}$ defined in Step~3.

\textit{Step 1.2: Second-order Thevenin ECM.} Let $V_t$ be the terminal voltage and $V_{oc}(z)$ the OCV. The cell is modelled by an ohmic resistance $R_0$ and two RC pairs with polarisation voltages $v_1$, $v_2$:
\begin{align}
    V_t &= V_{oc}(z) - R_0(T_b)\,I - v_1 - v_2, \label{eq:vt} \\
    \dot{v}_i &= -\frac{v_i}{R_i C_i} + \frac{I}{C_i}, \quad i = 1, 2. \label{eq:vi}
\end{align}
$R_0$ follows an Arrhenius law:
\begin{equation}
    R_0(T_b) = R_{0,\mathrm{ref}}
    \exp\!\left[\beta\!\left(\frac{1}{T_b + 273.15} - \frac{1}{298.15}\right)\right].
    \label{eq:arrhenius}
\end{equation}

\textit{Step 1.3: Constant-power load and implicit current solve.} The DC--DC converter imposes $P_{\mathrm{load}} = \eta_{\mathrm{dc}} V_t I$. Substituting \eqref{eq:vt} yields a quadratic in $I$:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{dc}} R_0(T_b)\, I^2
    - \eta_{\mathrm{dc}}\tilde{V}\, I
    + P_{\mathrm{load}} = 0,
    \quad
    \tilde{V} \triangleq V_{oc}(z) - v_1 - v_2.
    \label{eq:quad}
\end{equation}
Let $\Delta_I \triangleq (\eta_{\mathrm{dc}}\tilde{V})^2 - 4\eta_{\mathrm{dc}} R_0 P_{\mathrm{load}}$. The smaller non-negative root is taken:
\begin{equation}
    I(t) = \frac{\eta_{\mathrm{dc}}\tilde{V} - \sqrt{\max(\Delta_I,\, 0)}}{2\eta_{\mathrm{dc}} R_0(T_b)}.
    \label{eq:iroot}
\end{equation}
When $\Delta_I < 0$ at very low SOC, a numerical floor $V_{\min}$ is applied to $\tilde{V}$, distinct from the shutdown cutoff $V_{\mathrm{cut}}$ (Step~4).

\textbf{Step 2: Component-level power decomposition.}
Let $\mathcal{K} = \{\mathrm{scr},\,\mathrm{cpu},\,\mathrm{wifi},\,\mathrm{cell},\,\mathrm{gps},\,\mathrm{audio},\,\mathrm{flash}\}$ be the set of hardware components. Load power is decomposed additively:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{load}}(t) = P_{\mathrm{base}}
    + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \alpha_k\, x_k(t)
    + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \epsilon_k(t),
    \quad \epsilon_k(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,\sigma_k^2),
    \label{eq:pdecomp}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ is the always-on baseline, $x_k(t) \in [0, 1]$ is the normalised activity of component $k$, $\alpha_k$ is the per-component power coefficient, and $\epsilon_k(t)$ models OS scheduling jitter. Coefficients $\{\alpha_k\}$ are fitted from ADB \texttt{batterystats} traces (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}). The stochastic terms propagate through \eqref{eq:quad} and \eqref{eq:soc}, yielding a TTE uncertainty interval.

\textbf{Step 3: Temperature and aging correction.}

\textit{Temperature--capacity multiplier.}
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\mathrm{temp}}(T_b)
    = \mathrm{clip}\!\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_{n=0}^{3} c_n T_b^n,\;
      \phi_{\min},\;\phi_{\max}\Bigr).
    \label{eq:phitemp}
\end{equation}

\textit{Cycle-aging multiplier.} Capacity fade follows an exponential decay:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\mathrm{age}}(N, T_b) = \exp\!\bigl(-k(T_b)\, N\bigr),
    \quad k(T_b) = k_0 + k_1 T_b,
    \label{eq:phiage}
\end{equation}
where $k_0$, $k_1$ are fitted to the NASA battery aging dataset~\cite{nasa_dataset}.

\textit{Thermal dynamics.} Battery temperature evolves via a lumped heat balance~\cite{bernardi1985}:
\begin{equation}
    m c_p \frac{dT_b}{dt}
    = Q_{\mathrm{gen}}(t) - hA\bigl(T_b(t) - T_{\mathrm{amb}}\bigr),
    \label{eq:thermal}
\end{equation}
with Joule heating:
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = I^2 R_0(T_b) + \frac{v_1^2}{R_1} + \frac{v_2^2}{R_2}.
    \label{eq:qgen}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4: Debounced end-of-discharge criterion.}
Let $V_{\mathrm{cut}}$ be the OS-enforced shutdown voltage. To avoid premature termination from transient dips, $V_t$ is low-pass filtered before thresholding:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{v}_f = \frac{V_t - v_f}{\tau_f}.
    \label{eq:lpf}
\end{equation}
Discharge ends at $t_{\mathrm{cut}} = \min\{\,t \mid v_f(t) \leq V_{\mathrm{cut}}\,\}$, and TTE from initial time $t_0$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TTE} = t_{\mathrm{cut}} - t_0.
    \label{eq:tte_def}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5: Coupled ODE integration and TTE inference.}
Collecting \eqref{eq:soc}--\eqref{eq:lpf}, the system state is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(t) = \bigl[z(t),\; v_1(t),\; v_2(t),\; T_b(t),\; v_f(t)\bigr]^\top,
    \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
governed by the coupled nonlinear ODE:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
    -I / Q_{\max,\mathrm{eff}} \\[3pt]
    -v_1/(R_1 C_1) + I/C_1 \\[3pt]
    -v_2/(R_2 C_2) + I/C_2 \\[3pt]
    \bigl[Q_{\mathrm{gen}} - hA(T_b - T_{\mathrm{amb}})\bigr]/(m c_p) \\[3pt]
    (V_t - v_f)/\tau_f
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:odesys}
\end{equation}
where $I(t)$ is resolved via \eqref{eq:iroot} at each evaluation. Given input $\mathcal{I}(t_0)$ from \eqref{eq:input_tuple}, the inference loop at each step $\Delta t$ is:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[1)] Compute $P_{\mathrm{load}}(t)$ from \eqref{eq:pdecomp}.
    \item[2)] Update $R_0(T_b)$ via \eqref{eq:arrhenius}.
    \item[3)] Solve for $I(t)$ via \eqref{eq:iroot}.
    \item[4)] Advance $\mathbf{x}$ by $\Delta t$ using forward Euler.
    \item[5)] If $v_f \leq V_{\mathrm{cut}}$, terminate and return $\mathrm{TTE}$ from \eqref{eq:tte_def}.
\end{enumerate}
The solver additionally returns per-component energy attributions
\begin{equation}
    E_k = \int_{t_0}^{t_{\mathrm{cut}}} \alpha_k\, x_k(\tau)\, d\tau,
    \quad k \in \mathcal{K},
    \label{eq:energy_attr}
\end{equation}
for direct diagnosis of runtime bottlenecks.





\section{Experiment Setup}
\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Data Collection}

Power traces were collected from two Android smartphones: Xiaomi~14~Pro (4880~mAh) and Huawei~Mate~9~Pro (4000~mAh). On each device, the Android Debug Bridge (ADB) \texttt{batterystats} interface was used to log per-component power estimates, SOC, voltage, and temperature at \SI{1}{min} intervals. Three usage profiles were recorded, each spanning a full discharge cycle from \SI{100}{\percent} SOC to automatic shutdown:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[1)] \textit{Video}: simulated browsing of short-video feeds (TikTok-style) at \SI{50}{\percent} screen brightness.
    \item[2)] \textit{Gaming}: 3DMark benchmark running continuously at \SI{80}{\percent} brightness.
    \item[3)] \textit{Mixed}: alternating cycles of web browsing, video playback, GPS navigation, and idle, simulating typical daily use.
\end{enumerate}

For each device--profile pair, three full discharge traces were recorded, yielding 18 traces in total. Two traces per pair (12 traces) were used for parameter fitting; the remaining one per pair (6 traces) formed the held-out test set.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $\mathrm{TTE}_i$ and $\widehat{\mathrm{TTE}}_i$ denote the ground-truth and predicted TTE for trace $i$, respectively, and let $n$ be the number of test traces. Three metrics are used:

\textit{Local TTE MAE} (minutes). At each prediction time $t$ within a trace, the local TTE is computed via \eqref{eq:tte_local} and compared against the ground-truth remaining time. The mean over all prediction instants across all traces is reported:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{TTE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_i|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_i} \bigl|\widehat{\mathrm{TTE}}_{i}(t) - \mathrm{TTE}_{i}(t)\bigr|,
    \label{eq:mae_tte}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_i$ is the set of prediction instants for trace $i$.

\textit{Local TTE MAPE} (\%). The mean absolute percentage error of TTE, defined analogously:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAPE}_{\mathrm{TTE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_i|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_i} \frac{|\widehat{\mathrm{TTE}}_{i}(t) - \mathrm{TTE}_{i}(t)|}{\mathrm{TTE}_{i}(t)} \times 100\%.
    \label{eq:mape_tte}
\end{equation}

\textit{SOC MAE} (percentage points). The mean absolute deviation between the predicted SOC trajectory $\widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_i(t)$ and the ground-truth SOC:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{SOC}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_i|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_i} \bigl|\widehat{\mathrm{SOC}}_{i}(t) - \mathrm{SOC}_{i}(t)\bigr|.
    \label{eq:mae_soc}
\end{equation}

For all metrics, lower values indicate better performance. Local TTE MAE is the primary metric.






\section{Results Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate the proposed model on three usage profiles (video, gaming, mixed) across two devices. The simulation uses a time step of $\Delta t = \SI{1}{\second}$, $V_{\mathrm{cut}} = \SI{3.3}{\volt}$, and a debounce filter constant $\tau_f = \SI{30}{\second}$. All metrics are reported on the held-out test set defined in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.

On Huawei Gaming, Smartphone-DL-adaptive achieves a lower MAE (\SI{0.197}{h} vs.\ \SI{0.241}{h}), likely because the DNN overfits to the repetitive periodic pattern of the game workload on that device; however, the same DNN fails catastrophically on Xiaomi Gaming (\SI{1.637}{h}) and on video profiles (\SI{2.215}{h}--\SI{4.073}{h}), highlighting the brittleness of data-driven methods under distributional shift. In contrast, the physics-based structure of our model yields consistent performance across devices and profiles.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

To quantify which parameters most influence TTE, we compute the dimensionless elasticity
\begin{equation}
    S_p = \frac{\partial \ln \mathrm{TTE}}{\partial \ln p}
        = \frac{p}{\mathrm{TTE}}\frac{\partial \mathrm{TTE}}{\partial p},
    \label{eq:elasticity}
\end{equation}
for each multiplicative parameter $p$ in the model.

Fig.~\ref{fig:elasticity} ranks $S_p$ across profiles. The dominant levers are nominal capacity $Q_{\max}$, the screen power coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{scr}}$, the CPU coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{cpu}}$, and the baseline draw $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. Network-related coefficients ($\alpha_{\mathrm{wifi}}$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{cell}}$) rank moderately, while GPS, audio, and Bluetooth coefficients are consistently negligible. A complementary $\pm 10\%$ tornado perturbation under the gaming profile (Fig.~\ref{fig:tornado}) corroborates this ranking through a direct sensitivity approach: screen and CPU perturbations produce the largest TTE swings ($\pm\SI{0.4}{h}$--\SI{0.6}{h}), whereas flashlight and Bluetooth perturbations are within $\pm\SI{0.02}{h}$.

\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we proposed a hybrid physics-informed model for smartphone time-to-empty prediction that couples a second-order Thevenin ECM with component-level power decomposition and temperature/aging modulation. Unlike prior work that models the battery cell in isolation, the proposed framework jointly models peripheral components (screen, CPU, network radios, GPS) whose power draw dominates runtime discharge. The model parameters are fitted from authentic data collected via ADB \texttt{batterystats} on two real Android devices across three usage profiles.

Experimental results on held-out test traces demonstrate that the proposed model achieves a mean TTE MAE of \SI{0.474}{h}, outperforming the best baseline in five of six scenarios with an average reduction of \SI{16.7}{\percent}. The largest gains appear on video and mixed workloads, where the physics-informed ECM provides a structural advantage over purely statistical extrapolation. Ablation experiments confirm that both temperature correction and ECM dynamics contribute non-trivially, with their relative importance varying by workload: temperature correction is essential under wide thermal swings, while RC dynamics are most beneficial under rapidly fluctuating GPU loads. Sensitivity analysis further reveals that screen and CPU power coefficients are the dominant TTE levers and should be calibrated per device, whereas GPS and audio coefficients can be transferred across devices with negligible accuracy loss.

Future work will extend the model to capture battery aging effects from longitudinal user traces and incorporate per-user workload prediction to enable proactive power management on consumer smartphones.
